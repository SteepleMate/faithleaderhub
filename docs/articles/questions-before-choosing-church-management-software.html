Ten Questions to Ask Before Choosing Church Management Software

November 18, 2026

Choosing church management software is one of those decisions that seems simple until you're in the middle of it. Every product has a polished demo, and most of them can do more than your church will ever need. The harder question is whether a system fits the way your congregation actually works.

Before you compare features, gather two or three people who will use the system most: the administrator, the pastor, and perhaps a ministry leader or treasurer. Then work through these questions together.

1. What problem are we actually trying to solve?

Write it down in one or two sentences. "We lose track of first-time guests." "Our volunteer schedule lives in three spreadsheets." "We can't find anyone's current phone number." A clear problem keeps you from being dazzled by features you'll never use.

2. Who will use it, and how comfortable are they with technology?

A system your administrator loves but your volunteers can't figure out will quietly fail. Think about your least technical regular user. Can they log in, find what they need and update their own information without a phone call to the office?

3. Does it work well on a phone?

Many of your members and volunteers will only ever use the system from a phone. Check the experience for checking in a child, signing up for a serving slot or updating an address on a small screen, not just on the administrator's desktop.

4. How will we get our existing data in?

Ask exactly how importing works. Can you bring in your current member list from a spreadsheet? What about groups, attendance history or giving records? Find out whether the company helps with this, and whether there's a cost.

5. Can we get our data back out?

This question is easy to skip and important to ask. If you ever change systems, you'll want a full, usable export of your people and records. A trustworthy vendor will answer this plainly.

6. Who can see what?

Church records include sensitive information: home addresses, children's details, pastoral care notes and giving history. Ask how permissions work. Can you limit a nursery volunteer to check-in only? Can you keep pastoral notes visible to the pastor alone?

7. What does it really cost?

Look past the headline price. Ask about:

  • Charges based on the number of people in your database
  • Add-on modules for things like check-in, texting or websites
  • Fees for online giving or payment processing
  • Setup, training or data migration charges

Then picture your church in three years, a little larger, and estimate the cost again.

8. What happens when we need help?

Find out how support works. Is it email only, or can you talk to a person? Are there help articles and videos? Is support included in the price? If possible, ask another church of similar size about their experience.

9. Will it replace other tools, or add another one?

The goal is usually fewer places to look, not more. Make a list of what you use today: the calendar, the email tool, the check-in system, the volunteer spreadsheet. See which of these the new system could reasonably replace, and which would still need to live somewhere else.

10. Can we try it with real work before we commit?

A demo shows you the best version of a product. A trial shows you your own week. If a trial is offered, use it on an ordinary task: plan next Sunday's volunteers, enter a few new guests, or set up a small group. Ask the people who will use it daily what they think.

A few words on the decision itself

Give yourselves enough time. Rushing into a system because the old one is painful often trades one set of problems for another. At the same time, don't wait for a perfect option. No system will match every preference, and a good-enough tool that people actually use is worth more than a perfect one that sits idle.

When you do choose, plan a simple rollout. Start with the core: people and contact information. Add groups, check-in and scheduling once the basics are steady. Train a few volunteers first, and let them help others.

For contracts, data protection requirements or anything involving financial records, it's wise to have your attorney or CPA look over the terms.

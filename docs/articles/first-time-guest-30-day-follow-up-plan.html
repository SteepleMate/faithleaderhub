A 30-Day Follow-Up Plan for First-Time Guests

November 18, 2026

Walking into a church for the first time takes courage. A guest doesn't know where to park, where the bathrooms are, or whether anyone will notice them. When someone takes that step, the most loving thing a church can do is make sure they feel seen, and then follow up in a way that's warm without being pushy.

Good follow-up isn't a sales funnel. It's hospitality that continues after Sunday. Here is a simple 30-day plan that a small team can keep.

Before anything else: make it easy to connect

Follow-up depends on knowing who visited. Offer a simple way for guests to share their information:

  • A short connection card in the pew or at the welcome table
  • A sign or slide with a link to a brief online form
  • A friendly greeter who offers to help, rather than a clipboard

Ask only for what you'll actually use: name, a way to contact them, and perhaps whether they'd like a call or have children. Every extra question makes it less likely they'll fill it out. And let them know how you'll use their information.

Day 1 (Sunday): A warm welcome

The first follow-up happens in person.

  • Greet guests by name if they've shared it, and introduce them to one or two people.
  • Offer a small, simple welcome gift if your church does that.
  • Walk them to the children's area or a class rather than pointing.

Don't overwhelm them. One genuine conversation is better than five rushed handshakes.

Days 1-2: A personal thank-you

Within a day or two, send a short note. A handwritten card from the pastor or a welcome team member is lovely; a personal email is fine too. Thank them for coming, mention something specific if you can, and let them know you'd be glad to see them again. Keep it short, and don't include a long list of programs.

Days 3-7: A friendly contact

If the guest indicated they'd welcome it, have someone reach out by phone or message. This is a chance to ask how their visit went and whether they have questions. Listen more than you talk. If they mention a need, such as a child who was nervous in the nursery or a recent move, write it down and pass it to the right person.

If they didn't want a call, respect that. A second short email with practical information (service times, parking, what's happening for kids) is enough.

Days 8-14: An invitation to something small

Many people connect through a smaller setting before they feel at home in a whole congregation. Invite them to one specific thing that fits what you've learned about them:

  • A newcomers' lunch or coffee with the pastor
  • A small group or Bible study meeting nearby
  • A serving opportunity that doesn't require a long commitment
  • A family event if they have children

One clear invitation works better than a calendar of options.

Days 15-30: Notice and stay in touch

By now, you'll know whether the guest has returned. Either way, keep caring.

  • If they've come back, welcome them by name and connect them with someone who shares an interest or stage of life.
  • If they haven't, a single gentle note is appropriate: you're glad they visited, and the door is always open.
  • At the end of the month, mark where things stand so no one is contacted too often or forgotten entirely.

After about 30 days, guests who are returning can move into your regular care and connection rhythm. Those who haven't returned should stop receiving follow-up messages unless they ask for more.

Keeping the plan running

A plan like this only works if someone owns it. Choose one person, staff or volunteer, to watch the list each week. Keep a simple record of each guest: when they visited, who contacted them and what was said. That way, two people don't call the same family, and no one is missed.

Pray for your guests by name as a team. It keeps the focus where it belongs: on people, not process.

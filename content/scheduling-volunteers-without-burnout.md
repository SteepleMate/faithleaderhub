Every church depends on volunteers, and nearly every church has a few people who carry far more than their share. They're the ones who say yes when no one else does, and they're often the first to quietly step away when it all becomes too much.

Scheduling well is one of the most practical ways to care for the people who serve. Here is how to build a schedule that spreads the load and doesn't leave you rebuilding it every Thursday night.

## Start with the roles, not the people

Before you fill a single slot, list every role you need for a typical week:

- Greeters and ushers
- Nursery and children's teachers
- Worship team and tech (sound, slides, livestream)
- Readers, communion servers, coffee and hospitality
- Setup and teardown

For each role, write down how many people it needs, what training or background check it requires, and how long a shift lasts. This simple list often reveals roles you can combine, simplify or retire.

## Ask people how often they want to serve

The most common cause of burnout is serving more often than someone expected. Instead of guessing, ask. When someone joins a team, have a short conversation:

- How often would you like to serve: weekly, twice a month, monthly?
- Are there weeks or seasons you're usually away?
- Would you be willing to be a backup?

Write down their answers and honor them. If a person agreed to monthly, don't schedule them three weeks in a row because it's convenient.

## Build teams that rotate

Rotating teams are easier to manage than individuals. Group volunteers into teams that serve on a regular cycle, for example Team A on the first Sunday, Team B on the second, and so on. People get to know their teammates, swaps happen within the team, and you can see at a glance who's on.

Keep a small pool of willing backups for each role. Knowing who to ask first when someone is sick saves a lot of last-minute texting.

## Publish the schedule early

Share the schedule at least a month ahead when you can. People plan their lives around it, and an early schedule gives them time to tell you about conflicts. A reminder a few days before their shift helps too, especially for people who serve monthly and may lose track.

Make it easy to swap. Volunteers should know exactly how to trade a date or ask for a replacement, and the person who coordinates the team should hear about it.

## Watch for the warning signs

Burnout rarely announces itself. Watch for:

- The same few names showing up on several teams
- Someone who used to arrive early now arriving just in time
- More frequent cancellations or "I'm just tired" comments
- Leaders who never get to sit in worship themselves

When you see these, have a gentle conversation. Offer a break without guilt. A season of rest often brings people back stronger than pushing them through.

## Give people a real Sabbath

Encourage every volunteer, especially leaders, to take regular weeks off from serving so they can simply worship. Build those weeks into the schedule rather than hoping they happen. Rest is part of a healthy rhythm, not a failure of commitment.

## Say thank you, often and specifically

Appreciation doesn't need a budget. A handwritten note, a mention from the pulpit, or a simple "the nursery was so peaceful this morning because of you" goes a long way. Thank people for what they did, not just for being volunteers in general.

## Getting out of the spreadsheet

Spreadsheets are a fine place to start, but they get harder to manage as teams grow. Common problems include several versions floating around, no automatic reminders, and no easy way for volunteers to see their own dates or request a swap. If you spend more time maintaining the schedule than talking with your volunteers, it may be time for a tool built for the job.

For questions about background checks, child safety policies or volunteer-related insurance, check with your attorney and your church's insurance provider.
